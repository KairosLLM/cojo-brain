A 23,000 payroll print, and the week the two of them finally contradicted each other

2026-08-09 · sunday

Synthesized from what Benjamin Cowen and Jordi Visser published this week. They have not spoken to each other and did not participate. Quotes are verbatim and linked; everything else is labeled paraphrase.

Written August 10, 2026, the day after the week closed. It uses only material published on or before August 9 and market prices through that date.

  • Ben: the S&P correction starts by late September at the latest, and Bitcoin goes below 60k before the cycle low is in.
  • Jordi: the crash is over, the bottom is in, and he is shocked anyone still prices a September hike.
  • The tape: a 23,000 payroll print took September hike odds from 57.5% to 35.5%, fifteen points of it in a single hour.
  • The record: Ben 1-0 with 9 open. Jordi 0-1 with 9 open. Scorecard

The pitch

Ben spent five videos putting a date on the same forecast, and by Saturday he had the mechanism too. The clearest statement came after the payroll report:

"I would look for a correction to start by late September at the latest in line with prior midterm years, 2014, 2018, 2022."

— Ben, August 8, 25:31

He built that out of three dates. The 2014 correction started September 19. The 2018 correction started September 21. The 2022 top was August 16. The next Fed meeting is September 16. On Tuesday, with the S&P at 7750 and making new all-time highs, he laid out the fork:

"You're looking at about a 57% chance of a rate hike, a 43% chance that they do absolutely nothing."

— Ben, August 4, 6:09

And he made the fork not matter:

"If the Fed were not to raise rates in September, then the argument would simply be that the long end of the yield curve is going to go higher. You're likely going to have the 10-year yield breaking out above 5%."

— Ben, August 4, 7:42

Hike and equities de-rate on fear the easing cycle is over. No hike and the long end revolts. Either branch gets him his correction.

On Bitcoin he is now working a squeeze, not a level. Monday's video is eight minutes long and has one idea in it. The bear market resistance band is coming down through 69k, the 200-week moving average is coming up through 63.7k, and price is between them at 63.5k:

"Are we going to break above the bear market resistance band, or are we going to break below the 200-week moving average?"

— Ben, August 3, 1:36

He says which way he expects it to break:

"I can't begin to tell you, you know, exactly what week it's going to occur before Bitcoin likely heads back down below 60K."

— Ben, August 3, 3:10

The seasonal case behind it is the same one he has run all year, and this week he gave the full ledger:

"every prior midterm year was red for August. Negative 15% in 2022, negative 15% in 2018, negative 18% in 2014, even in 2010, negative 5%."

— Ben, August 3, 5:28

Thursday's cycle video adds the timing. Bitcoin is on day 1354 measured from the 2022 low, against cycles that ran 1430 to 1440 days, and on day 304 of a bear market that has averaged 406 days from the peak.

"my guess is that Bitcoin will bottom when it normally bottoms"

— Ben, August 6, 12:24

That window, in his words, is "late September through mid December," and his summary of the whole exercise is that

"I would say we are in the back 30% of the of the bare market."

— Ben, August 6, 17:49

Then Friday's jobs report arrived and Ben spent 28 minutes explaining why it does not mean what it looks like. Non-farm payrolls came in at 23,000 against roughly 80,000 expected, and the unemployment rate fell anyway, to 4.1% from 4.5% last November. His resolution:

"it is true that you don't have as many jobs, but there's also not as many people looking for a job."

— Ben, August 8, 7:01

Labor force participation has fallen from 62.5% to 61.4% since November. Paraphrasing the rest of his read: layoffs are only back at pre-pandemic levels, initial claims printed 189,000 a few weeks ago, and the weakness is a hiring freeze rather than a firing cycle. His line in the sand:

"as long as initial claims are below 300K, we're just not in a recession."

— Ben, August 8, 23:11

He also watched his own Fed number move against him inside four days, and said so:

"There's only about a 43.9% chance as it stands right now that you're going to get a rate hike in September."

— Ben, August 8, 17:49

Two smaller Cowen calls that are easy to miss and are both scoreable. On gold, down about 30% from its high, he refuses to turn:

"if the bull market's intact, which I still think it is, not financial advice, I still think it is, then the June to October time frame is when you would expect a bottom to occur."

— Ben, August 5, 6:57

And on the dollar, which is the hinge under everything else he said this week:

"the dollar in 2026 looks a lot like the dollar in 2018 where it just kind of begrudgingly goes higher."

— Ben, August 5, 5:27

Jordi published the week's most ambitious piece of writing on Tuesday and its most consequential call on Sunday. Take the call first, because it is the one that can be graded:

"It's over. The bottom is in as far as I'm concerned on a probability basis. If we take out the lows, I'd be surprised."

— Jordi, August 9, 13:56

The evidence he wanted was follow-through, and he got it. The four-day rally on the Nasdaq ran close to 10%. The S&P was up 3.6% on the week and made new all-time highs, as did the equal-weight S&P, the Russell, the NYSE composite, the DAX and the FTSE. Seven-day realized volatility on the tech momentum factor collapsed. Paraphrasing his read of that collapse: falling factor vol means funds are hedged or have cut gross, anyone vol-targeting cannot re-lever, it is August and volume is thin, and everyone who is short this is about to be forced to chase.

Out of the same observation he made a structural call:

"I'm making the call that we are at peak gross leverage and that we will see leverage come down over the course of the next few years"

— Jordi, August 9, 15:28

On the Fed he is not just leaning the other way from Ben, he thinks the market is being silly.

"I'm shocked after this last two weeks that we still have a 45% chance of a rate hike in September."

— Jordi, August 9, 38:33

His mechanism is not the labor market. It is Japan. The US and Japan intervened jointly to support the yen, the first coordinated move since 1998, and Bessent is reportedly leaning on the Fed to expand the facility behind it. Paraphrasing his argument: if the Treasury needs the interest rate differential to narrow to keep Japan from selling treasuries, and it is asking the Fed for help to do it, a Fed hike works against the thing the Treasury just spent its ammunition on. He puts a larger frame on it:

"when the US is intervening in the Japanese currency market to help their treasury market, the fiat system might be in trouble"

— Jordi, August 9, 40:51

Which is how he gets to the trade:

"Gold's in a bull market."

— Jordi, August 9, 43:09

"the dollar's already broken down, but this is just fuel for the fire for gold and and silver and platinum and Bitcoin."

— Jordi, August 9, 43:54

Gold had its third-biggest week in sixteen years, up 7.2%, and closed back above its 50-day after the third-longest stretch below it on his data.

And on the labor market he is the mirror image of Ben, using Ben's own facts.

"Yes, there are low claims and the reason there's low claims, part of this is the labor force is coming down, but they never talk about that."

— Jordi, August 9, 45:25

"You can't tell me the labor market is good with those metrics."

— Jordi, August 9, 46:14

The metrics are the quits rate and Atlanta Fed wage growth back at 2019 levels, average hourly earnings at 3.2%, and aggregate weekly payrolls printing

"the weakest six-month rate of change on the aggregate weekly payrolls going all the way back to 2012 when you throw out the COVID time period"

— Jordi, August 9, 46:59

His causal story is AI. Paraphrasing: agents have already hit hiring and have not yet hit firing, the job creation is happening inside one-person AI-native companies that do not show up in the establishment survey, and healthcare is the only sector adding jobs at all.

The Tuesday paper is the best thing either of them published this week and it opens nothing scoreable inside the year. Worth saying plainly, because the ledger is what this archive is for. The Age of Abundant Intelligence and Scarce Bitcoin takes one observation, that factor volatility hit all-time highs in July while Bitcoin's realized volatility sat at cycle lows, and builds a regime out of it:

"The asset class built on forecastable cash flows turned violent. The asset with no cash flows at all went quiet."

— Jordi, August 4

The thesis compresses to four words:

"Fiat printing debases the money. AI printing debases the moat."

— Jordi, August 4

Paraphrasing the argument between those two lines: AI is compressing the duration of every corporate moat, which widens the confidence interval around terminal value, which raises the effective discount rate on distant cash flows even for companies whose current earnings never miss. That is why the doubt climbed from software to the model labs to the hyperscalers in a year while Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon were carrying roughly $1.7 trillion of contracted forward demand. His conclusion is that scarcity that no press can reach gets re-rated, and that

"Liquidity is an option on time."

— Jordi, August 4

It is a genuinely good paper. It also has no dates in it, which is why the claims below come from the video and not from the paper.

The backtrack

Nine claims were open going into this week. One is graded, and it is graded early.

Jordi, July 26: no straight-line recovery in tech momentum, and the second half is chop. Missed. He said it after an 8% momentum bounce week:

"I find very little chance that we go straight back up. If we did go back up, I think it's going to take some time. I think it'll take the whole second half of the year."

— Jordi, July 26, 3:03

Eight trading days later the Nasdaq had rallied close to 10% in four days, his own thematic portfolio was up 6.5% in its second-biggest week of the year, and the S&P, the equal-weight S&P, the Russell, the NYSE composite, the DAX and the FTSE were all at new all-time highs. It went straight back up. The honest counter is that the horizon runs to December 31 and the second half could still turn choppy from here. That counter is being rejected because the operative content of the claim was the mechanism, not the calendar, and because the man who made it spent Sunday declaring the bottom in. Waiting until December to record that the first leg was wrong would be the exact thing this ledger exists to prevent. (2026-07-26-visser-01)

Jordi, August 2: the late-July unwind was a cleansing event, not the start of a sustained decline. Still open, horizon October 31, and aging very well. The follow-through he said he needed arrived in the first two sessions of the week and held. Factor volatility collapsed, the VIX went to its lowest since January, and CDX did nothing at all. This is the claim most likely to land of anything on the board. (2026-08-02-visser-01)

Jordi, August 2: realized volatility on the tech momentum factor stays structurally above its pre-2026 highs near 70. Still open, horizon December 31, and it is now fighting his own chart. Seven-day realized vol on tech momentum collapsed this week, and he showed it, approvingly, as the evidence the bottom was in. He restated the structural version anyway, saying he thinks factor vol does not go back down and that the rest of the year probably sits in a 25 to 30 band on his four-factor composite. That band is on a different scale from the 70 in the claim, so nothing is falsified yet, but the claim needs a specific measure named or it will be ungradeable in December. (2026-08-02-visser-02)

Jordi, August 1: rising leverage into falling terminal-value confidence is structural, and gross exposure does not return to prior highs. Still open, horizon December 31. He doubled down this week and put a name on it, calling peak gross leverage outright. Consistent, and now paired with a sharper claim below. (2026-08-01-visser-03)

Jordi, July 25: the high-multiple phase of the AI trade is finished. Still open, horizon December 31. A week in which his thematic basket posted its second-biggest gain of the year is not evidence against a claim about 7-8x returns, but it is not evidence for it either. Nothing to score. (2026-07-25-visser-02)

Ben, July 26: Bitcoin is rejected at the bear market resistance band and trades lower through August and September. Still open, horizon September 30. Bitcoin traded 63.5k on Monday and did not go near the band, which is now down at 69k. The rejection has not been tested. The band is falling toward price rather than price rising to the band, which is the squeeze his Monday video is about. (2026-07-26-cowen-01)

Ben, July 26: Bitcoin posts a red August. Still open, horizon September 1. One week in. Bitcoin was roughly flat and finished the week around the 200-week moving average. The synthesis

Last week they were watching two different transmission channels and both were right. This week they read the same jobs report and came out on opposite sides of four separate questions.

On the labor market they agree on the fact and split on the meaning, and the agreement is more surprising than the split. Both of them identify the same mechanism, in the same week, independently: the unemployment rate is falling because people are leaving the labor force, not because anyone is hiring. Ben says it Saturday. Jordi says it Sunday, and adds that nobody says it, which is a slightly awkward thing to say the day after Ben said it on a macro channel with a large audience. He is not responding to Ben and there is no evidence either watched the other. But from there they invert. Ben stacks the low layoffs, the 189,000 claims print and the state-level unemployment map against the payroll number and concludes the economy is not rolling over, which lets the market keep climbing the wall of worry. Jordi stacks wages back at 2019 levels and the weakest six-month aggregate payroll change since 2012 and concludes the labor market is already bad and everyone is measuring it wrong. Same numerator, opposite verdicts, and the tiebreaker is whichever one of them is right about what happens when a hiring freeze meets a rate decision.

On the Fed the gap is 20 points wide and both men are quoting the same instrument. Ben cited 57% for a September hike on Tuesday and 43.9% on Saturday. Jordi cited 45% on Friday and called it shockingly high. So Jordi's "absurdly overpriced" and Ben's "coin flip" are two readings of nearly the same number four days apart, and the difference is entirely about direction of travel. Ben watched it fall and treated it as noise around a meeting that still matters. Jordi watched it stay up and treated it as the market failing to process a currency intervention.

On the correction the two of them are now flatly incompatible, and this is the fork of the year. Ben has the drawdown ahead of him, starting by late September at the latest, arriving on schedule with the Fed meeting, and ushering in Bitcoin's cycle low. Jordi has the drawdown behind him, finished in July, with the bottom in and the lows unlikely to be retested. There is no reading under which both are right. If the S&P puts in a local top in the next six weeks and rolls, Ben's midterm-year template survives a year in which almost nothing else about the market was normal. If the index grinds up through September on collapsing factor volatility and thin August volume, Jordi's positioning read beats Ben's calendar and the template breaks for the first time in four midterm cycles.

The counter each has against the other is worth naming. Ben's protection is that his correction does not need leveraged sellers, only a higher discount rate, which is why the dollar and the long end matter more to him than positioning does. Jordi's protection is that the mechanical fuel has already been burned, and that the fiscal and currency arithmetic now forces the authorities to step in before yields can do the damage Ben needs them to do. That is his "nothing stops this fiscal train" argument, and it is why he can be structurally bearish on public companies out to 2030 and tactically long the tape at the same time.

On the dollar they are exactly opposite and nobody is hedging. Ben expects it to begrudgingly grind higher, the way it did in 2018, and treats that as the headwind that finally cracks risk assets late in the midterm year. Jordi says it has already broken down and that the break is fuel for gold, silver, platinum and Bitcoin. Everything else they disagree about routes through this one line, and it is the cleanest head-to-head in the archive so far.

On Bitcoin they agree on the diagnosis and disagree on the resolution, which is rarer and more useful. Both call it a bear market right now, in the same week, in almost the same words. Ben expects the squeeze between the 69k band and the 63.7k moving average to resolve downward, below 60k. Jordi expects it to hold and break out. Note the precision, because it matters at grading time: below-60k and a new cycle low are not the same event. Bitcoin's low this year is near 57k, so Ben's call can land without Jordi's failing.

One last thing worth holding onto. Ben's closing thought on the cold card exploit, made in the same week that over $100m of self-custodied Bitcoin was drained, was not about the exploit:

"I got to believe there's going to be something that's going to make this cycle look exactly the same as every other cycle."

— Ben, August 6, 40:10

That is the whole method in one sentence, and it is also the method's weakness. Every event gets absorbed into the template. The template has been right for a year.

The third voice

The crowd repriced the Fed harder in one hour than in any hour since the July meeting, and it repriced toward Jordi. September 25bp hike fell from 57.5% to 35.5%, down 22 points on the week, with -15.0 in a single hour at Friday 13:00Z, the first hourly bucket after the payroll release. Any hike in 2026 fell from 67.5% to 54.5%, with -7.0 in the same hour.

Note the level, because both men missed it. Ben quoted 43.9% on Saturday and Jordi quoted 45% on Friday, both from wire sources. Polymarket closed the week at 35.5%. Jordi's argument that the hike is badly overpriced is correct and understated: this market is nine points below the number he called shocking.

The bond market is where Ben got hurt, and the shape of the move is more interesting than the size. 10Y falls to 3.9% went from 5.9% to 19.9% on the week, but that understates it. It spiked +22.6 points in the hour at Friday 13:00Z, touching 27.2%, before giving most of it back. For about one hour the crowd priced an actual easing scenario, then thought better of it. That is a market discovering it has no idea what a 23,000 payroll print with a falling unemployment rate means, which is precisely the confusion Ben spent 28 minutes describing.

Meanwhile 10Y reaches 4.8% gave back 10.5 points. Two weeks ago that market went from 21% to 69.5% and looked like the archive's first big vindication. It is now 56.5%. Ben's 5% call is not dead, but the week ran against it.

The easing door reopened a crack for the first time since the July meeting. Zero cuts in 2026 slipped from 88.8% to 85.2% and one cut rose from 6.5% to 10.5%. Small numbers, but the direction reversed after four weeks of moving the other way. Neither man is positioned for a cut. Ben's whole frame is that the easing cycle is over and the argument is about hikes. Jordi's is that Warsh cannot hike, not that he will ease. If the crowd keeps walking this line up, both of them are looking at the wrong side of the distribution.

Bitcoin's distribution shifted up in a week both men called a bear market. The downside came off across the ladder: 60,000 from 80.5% to 76.5%, 45,000 from 24.5% to 20.5%. The upside went on: 70,000 to 72.5%, 80,000 to 35.5%. That is a modest bet against Ben's red August and a modest bet with Jordi's breakout, made in a week that included a nine-figure hardware wallet exploit and Saylor selling. Jordi noticed the same thing and said Bitcoin was okay this week despite the news flow. The crowd agrees with him.

And here is the line neither of them mentioned. Gold had its third-best week in sixteen years, up 7.2%, and Gold wins 2026 closed the week at 19.5%, exactly where it opened. It traded down to 17.5% midweek and came back. A 7% move in the underlying that produces zero change in the odds of it winning the year is the crowd saying this was a rate move, not a regime move. Jordi spent Sunday arguing it is a regime move. That is a clean, priced disagreement between him and the tape, and it is the cheapest way to be long his thesis.

One market that agrees with both of them. US recession by end of 2026 fell from 9.5% to 8.0% on a payroll miss, which is Ben's "we're just not in a recession" and Jordi's "I don't think we're ever going to see big job losses" priced in the same number.
